Family calendar · inside Home Assistant · 1.3

A family calendar
that stays at home.

Kev asked for a Google-like calendar in Home Assistant, for three people, each with their own account and with alerts, and above all secure. So it was built from scratch as a Home Assistant integration. It does what you'd expect from Google Calendar, and then some: where everyone has been, health from Withings, and calendars followed from other apps. Every event, password and position lives on the Pi in the house, behind a long list of protections. This page lists all of them.

01 · What it does

Everything you'd expect, and a bit more

It lives in the Home Assistant sidebar and on its own tab on the wall panel. It also installs on a phone like an app. Kev signs in with his Home Assistant account; Linda and the rest of the family have calendar-only logins, with no access to the rest of the house.

Six views
Day, 3 days, week, month, year and schedule. Drag to move or resize, and swipe between days on a phone. On the wall panel the day fits the screen, and it goes back to today after a few idle minutes.
Type it like you'd say it
"Lunch with Mum at the Swan 1:30pm every Saturday #family" fills in the time, the place, the repeat and the label. Repeating events can be edited for this one, this and following, or all.
Whose is it?
Each event wears its owner's initials in a little badge, with a border in their colour. Labels such as Work or Health give it a colour of their own, and can be hidden with one tap.
Where everyone has been
A thin strip down each day shows each person's stays and journeys. They come from the van's tracker and the phones. Name a place once and every visit there is labelled. A visit can become an event in one tap.
Health on the day
Weight, sleep score and steps from Withings appear under each day. Last night's sleep is drawn as a band across the hours. Each person picks what shows, and separately what the family may see.
Follow other calendars
Paste a webcal or https address, for example Stremio's new-episode calendar, a school's terms or a Google calendar. It stays up to date by itself and is read-only.
Alerts that reach you
Reminders pop up in the app, arrive as notifications on installed phones, and come through the Home Assistant app. Snooze works straight from the notification.
Live sync
A change on one screen appears on every other one in about a second. Undo works for ten minutes, and deleted events wait in a bin for thirty days.
Plays well with others
Import and export standard .ics files, bank holidays from gov.uk, and a documented API ready for an Android app later.

03 · Security, end to end

What a request has to get past

The calendar can be reached from outside the house, so every request passes the same series of checks before it touches any data. Who is asking is decided once, on the server. The app is never trusted to hide anything.

1 · transport
HTTPS only
Signing in is refused over plain http. The real client address is only believed when the request came through the house's own tunnel.
→
2 · who are you
Session or HA login
A calendar session cookie, a future app's token, or Kev's own Home Assistant login. Nothing else gets in.
→
3 · is it really you
Forgery checks
Every change must carry the app's own header and come from the calendar's own site.
→
4 · not too fast
Rate limits
Per person, per address and per password check.
→
5 · may you see it
Permissions
Sharing, private events, location and health rules, applied before anything is sent.
04 · Every measure

The full list

Grouped by what each measure protects against.

Getting in

  • Passwords are stored only as bcrypt hashes (cost 12), never readable. At least 10 characters. Common passwords and ones containing the person's name are refused.
  • Each person sets their own password from an invite link. The administrator never sees or sets anyone's password.
  • Two-step sign-in for calendar logins: a 6-digit authenticator code after the password (RFC 6238). Ten single-use recovery codes are stored only as hashes. A code can never be used twice, and the code step expires after 5 minutes or 5 wrong tries.
  • Account lockout: 5 wrong attempts lock the account for a minute, doubling each time up to an hour. Wrong two-step codes count too.
  • Address throttle: 30 failed sign-ins from one internet address in an hour blocks that address for an hour, whatever usernames it tries.
  • No username fishing: every wrong sign-in gets the same reply. Unknown usernames take the same time to answer and lock out exactly like real ones, so nothing reveals who has an account.
  • Invite and reset links work once and expire after 3 days. The secret is in the part of the link after the #, so it never reaches a server log, and only its fingerprint is stored. A new link also clears two-step, which is how someone who lost their phone gets back in.
  • Kev uses his Home Assistant login, so the calendar holds no separate password for him.

Staying signed in

  • The sign-in cookie (__Host-, HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Strict) can't be read by page scripts, only travels over https and isn't sent by other websites.
  • Session tokens are stored only as fingerprints (SHA-256), so a copy of the database contains no usable sign-ins.
  • Sessions expire after 90 days unused, or 365 for a future phone app.
  • Every signed-in device is listed and can be signed out on its own.
  • Changing a password signs out every other device. Switching a person off cuts them off immediately, including any live connection.
  • Signing out also removes that device's alert subscription.

Attacks through the browser

  • Forged changes: every change must carry a header only the calendar app sends, and is checked against a list of allowed origins. Another website can't act on your behalf.
  • The page's own rules (Content-Security-Policy) mean it may only run its own code and styles, connect only to its own server, and can't be framed by other sites.
  • Protective headers on everything: nosniff, no-referrer, same-origin opener and resource policies, and camera, microphone and location access switched off.
  • No third-party requests. Fonts, icons and code are all served from the Pi, so no outside service sees who opens the calendar.
  • The file server only hands out the app's own files (tested against ../ path tricks).
  • Text is always inserted as text, never as HTML, so an event title can't run code.

Flooding and overload

  • Per person: 300 requests and 60 changes a minute.
  • Password checks run at most two at a time, so a flood of sign-ins can't swamp the Pi.
  • At most 20 live connections per person.
  • Caps on everything: request size, text lengths, date ranges, import size, how many people, calendars, labels and places, and how many calendars one person can follow.

Who sees what

  • Enforced on the server, not hidden in the app: the app only ever receives what its user may see.
  • Private events reach others only as "Busy": no title, place, guests or labels. Search never matches inside them, export writes them as Busy, and free/busy shows only times.
  • Sharing per calendar and per person: none, free/busy, all details, or can edit.
  • Followed calendars are read-only for everyone, owner included.
  • Labels and named places can only be changed by whoever made them, or the administrator.

Location privacy

  • Coordinates never leave the Pi. The app only receives times, place names and distances.
  • Only the person can hide their location, or choose which of their devices show. Nobody else can change it, not even the administrator.
  • The administrator only links devices to people. He can't see or change their sharing.
  • Places are named privately with no outside map lookups. Home comes from Home Assistant itself.
  • Honest gaps: when a phone goes quiet the strip says "no signal" instead of guessing where someone was.
  • Phone-app positions are logged on the Pi and kept for 400 days.

Health privacy

  • Private by default. Nobody else sees any health data until its owner switches it on.
  • Sharing is one metric at a time, for example steps but not weight. "Show me" and "share with the family" are separate switches.
  • The data stays where it already was: the private Withings file on the Pi, read behind the login.

Followed calendars, safely

  • The feed address is a secret. Stremio's carries a private key to the account. It stays on the server: others see only the website name, and the owner a shortened form.
  • The house network can't be reached through it. Anyone with a login can add an address, so the server only ever fetches public internet addresses. It refuses home and private addresses, including the number and hex tricks for writing them, names that secretly point at a home address, and redirects to any of these. Every redirect is re-checked.
  • Strict limits: standard web ports only, 5 MB, 25 seconds, five redirects.
  • In-place sync: events keep the same identity from one refresh to the next, so reminders don't repeat and links don't break. Reminders are off unless the owner turns them on.

Data on the Pi

  • One database, private to Home Assistant: its folder and file can only be opened by HA itself.
  • Backed up automatically before every update, using the database's own safe online copy.
  • Upgrades never lose data: each new version upgrades the database in place and keeps everything in it, and the upgrade itself is tested.
  • Kept only as long as useful: bin 30 days, phone positions 400 days, security log 1 year, Undo 10 minutes (in memory only).

Alerts and the installed app

  • Alerts to installed apps are encrypted end to end (Web Push). Google or Apple only carry an envelope they can't open.
  • The app's offline copy holds the app only, never any calendar data.
  • Notifications open the calendar's own page, and Snooze works straight from them.
05 · Proof

Tested like an attacker would try it

56 automated tests are run before every update: 45 against the calendar's own logic and 11 against a real (test) Home Assistant over HTTP. They don't just check that features work. They try to break in.

What the tests attack

  • Requests without the app's header, or with no sign-in.
  • Guessing passwords until locked out, and checking that the lock looks the same for real and made-up usernames.
  • Reusing a two-step code, and using a recovery code twice.
  • Peeking at private events in the calendar views and through search.
  • Seeing someone's location after they hid it, as another person and as the administrator.
  • Feed addresses aimed at the house: plain home addresses, number and hex forms, IPv6 tricks, and a name whose lookup points home.
  • Path tricks against the file server, and upgrading old databases.

Caught before going live

The attack tests did their job. They caught two ways to slip a home address past the feed checker in the first draft, before it went live. A review also found that the sign-in lockout hinted which usernames were real, and that was fixed too. Each of these now has a test of its own, so it can't come back.

Every sign-in, failed attempt, invite, two-step change, device link and new subscription goes into a security log the administrator can read. It records the address each attempt came from.

Family calendar, at home

Convenient for the family, boring for an attacker.

The goal was a calendar the family enjoys using, where none of the data is anyone else's business: not an advertiser's, not a cloud's, and not a stranger's who found the sign-in page. The data lives on the Pi, the server decides who sees what, and the tests keep trying to break in.
